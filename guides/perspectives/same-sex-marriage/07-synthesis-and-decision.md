# Summary of the Six Arguments, and a Decision

*A synthesis of the six perspectives, followed by my own judgment on the question. The summaries aim to report each agent fairly; the decision section is my own reasoning and is labeled as such.*

---

## Part I — What Each Perspective Argued

### 01. Affirming, literary scholar
Deliberately narrow. He concedes at the outset that **no biblical author approved of same-sex relationships** and that no such voice is attested in antiquity. His claim is that the texts address other questions: Genesis 2:24 is marked syntactically as etiology (*'al-ken*), "one flesh" is kinship vocabulary before it is anatomical, Sodom is a hospitality-and-rape narrative confirmed as such by Ezekiel 16:49, Leviticus's *mishkevei ishah* idiom is bound up with status and penetration inside a boundary-drawing Holiness Code, Romans 1 is a rhetorical set-piece that springs at 2:1 and treats same-sex acts as a *consequence* of corporate idolatry, and *para physin* cannot bear metaphysical weight when Paul uses it of God in Romans 11:24. His constructive move is canonical: Isaiah 56 reverses Deuteronomy 23's exclusion of eunuchs, and Paul relativizes circumcision — an "everlasting" bodily command with a *karet* penalty. Boundary revision is a scriptural capacity, not an innovation.

**His stated weaknesses:** Leviticus 20:13's reciprocal liability fits an act-based prohibition better than his status reading; Romans 1:26 almost certainly includes female-female relations, which Brooten's work shows do not fit an exploitation frame; Matthew 19 has Jesus citing Genesis in a legal ruling; and eighteen centuries of unanimous reception is a heavy burden that "exegesis alone does not discharge."

### 02. Affirming, pastor
Argues that a marriage discipline including same-sex couples is **more faithful, not merely kinder**. Three pillars: (1) the fruit test of Matthew 7 applied honestly in both directions, where what he has watched the traditional teaching produce is closeted despair and mixed-orientation marriages he himself counseled people into; (2) marriage's load-bearing content in Ephesians 5 is self-gift, permanence and covenant fidelity — not procreation, which Paul does not mention; (3) celibacy in Matthew 19:11 and 1 Corinthians 7:7 is a *charism* distributed unevenly, so the traditional position requires asserting that God has certainly given a whole category of people a gift Jesus says is not given to everyone. His sharpest practical point: the real-world alternative to blessing is not celibacy but unaccountable relationship in the dark, so covenantal discipline is a *higher* sexual ethic than the one he was enforcing. He would lead by eighteen months of teaching on hermeneutics before ever addressing the issue, formally commission opponents to argue against him from his own pulpit, refuse to move without a supermajority, protect dissenters in writing, and disclose to his denomination before acting.

**What keeps him up:** James 3:1 — he may be using ordained authority to lay a stumbling block; fruit ripens over generations and he is reading a twenty-year sample with his thumb on the scale; every accommodation in history felt compassionate at the time; and the global church is against him, with no comfortable answer to the Nigerian brother who says this is colonialism with better manners.

### 03. Affirming, secular analyst
Concedes the exegesis and wins on method. **"Read flatly, the texts prohibit"** — including that *arsenokoitēs* most plausibly derives from LXX Leviticus, a point that favors traditionalists. But nothing transfers from an honor-shame, patriarchal, purity-coded world to a modern question without an interpretive bridge, and *both sides build one*. The dispute is whose bridge has fewer unsupported spans. He explicitly refuses the shellfish gotcha as a bad argument, and credits traditionalists with a real sorting principle: retain what is creation-grounded and reaffirmed across both testaments. His finding is that **divorce and remarriage satisfies that principle better than any same-sex text** — dominical, multiply attested, independently confirmed by Paul, creation-grounded — and is nonetheless not enforced, with the exegetical relaxation arriving after divorce became common among members. He counts auxiliary assumptions on each side and finds the affirming package leaner, and notes that affirming Christians generally admit they are doing developmental work while traditionalists exercise the same selectivity while describing themselves as simply reading.

**Best case against him, which he states:** the timing correlation is the strongest single traditionalist argument and is Bayesian rather than ad hominem; a tradition tracking the culture with a two-decade lag forfeits independent moral content; outcome data cannot settle a normative question; and the cleanest affirming position is "we have learned something our texts did not know," which most affirming Christians will not say because it costs them the authority they want.

### 04. Traditional, literary scholar
Builds a canonical spine rather than a proof-text pile. Genesis 1:27 places sexual difference *inside* the sentence defining the image of God; Genesis 2 solves not loneliness but the absence of a like-yet-other, and 2:24's "one flesh" is the rejoining of a division the narrative has just made. In Matthew 19 Jesus, asked only about divorce, cites **both** Genesis 1:27 and 2:24 — and 2:24 alone would have answered the question, making the male-female citation logically surplus and therefore telling. Leviticus 20:13's reciprocal liability tells against status, penetration and cult-prostitution readings alike, and the *qādēš* vocabulary a cultic reading needs is conspicuously absent. Romans 1 runs on Genesis diction (*arsen*/*thēly* rather than *andres*/*gynaikes*), and the female clause in 1:26 resists reduction to pederasty or slavery — a point established most thoroughly by Brooten, who is not a traditionalist. Second Temple Judaism is uniform, and the Greco-Roman world demonstrably *did* know non-exploitative forms, so "they only knew exploitation" is false. Acts 15 is a counter-example rather than a warrant: it dropped circumcision and retained *porneia*, mapping onto Leviticus 17–18 — the very chapters at issue.

**His stated weaknesses:** Leviticus keeps bad company and the Code itself does not label which prohibitions are trans-cultural, so the sorting is done by later Christian judgment; *arsenokoitai*'s referent is genuinely unknown, and if the derivation fails his New Testament vice-list evidence largely drops out; Romans 1 indicts his own use of it at 2:1; **Scripture nowhere addresses a covenanted, monogamous, mutual same-sex marriage**, so his case must run through inference, and inference is weaker than address; and the church has revised before while denying it was revising.

### 05. Traditional, pastor
Opens by conceding what his side usually will not: the church's treatment of gay people has been **a sin requiring confession and restitution**, not a matter of tone. He repudiates conversion therapy as a lie that does documented harm, family rejection as a grave sin, and the singling out of this sin while congregations metabolize greed and serial divorce. And he says the hardest sentence in the collection: none of that changes what he believes Scripture requires. His authority is delegated, not legislative; Jeremiah's "peace, peace, where there is no peace" indicts *physicians*, not enemies, and James 3:1 judges him in both directions — for softening what God said, and for every burden he laid on a back God never laid it on. He refuses the dishonest version of the celibacy analogy: the widow had her marriage, the straight single person's waiting has a logically possible end, and what he asks of a nineteen-year-old is a closed door forever. He then itemizes the debts his position incurs and which his side has almost universally refused to pay — household belonging, a serious theology of friendship, leadership roles, financial and end-of-life provision, protection from congregational cruelty — concluding that **the ethic is unlivable substantially because the church made it so**. He would baptize and commune a married same-sex couple, would not ordain one, would not officiate, and told a mother so before asking whether he could sit at the back of her daughter's wedding.

**What keeps him up:** he asks of others a cost he does not bear; the fruit he actually observes is more despair and quiet departure than flourishing celibacy — "Marta is real. So are the eleven others who are gone"; the church was certain about slavery and usury too and its defenders sounded exactly like him; "we love you but you may never have a partner" may be indistinguishable from rejection to the person receiving it; and he may be defending an institution he has a career in.

### 06. Traditional, secular analyst
Supports same-sex civil marriage without reservation and makes no moral defense of the traditional ethic. His claim is purely descriptive: **the affirming reading is a revision of the tradition, not a recovery of it.** Leviticus 20:13's reciprocity, Romans 1:26's female clause, and the LXX derivation of *arsenokoitai* point one way, with honest flags on where evidence is thin. He regards reception history as decisive for the descriptive question, and offers the sharpest single datum in the collection: **John Chrysostom** was a native Greek speaker for whom Greek pederasty was cultural memory rather than reconstruction, and he discusses the women of Romans 1:26 and reads the passage generally — so if a linguistic and cultural nuance was lost, one must explain why it was lost on the people who had the language and the culture. On the correlation between affirming exegesis and prior cultural shift he grants the abolition parallel at full strength, then distinguishes it: the strongest antislavery Christians largely argued for *override* rather than reinterpretation; slavery and usury had genuine pre-modern minority reports and this has none; and the affirming reading travels along lines of prior acceptance rather than philological merit. He judges Boswell's *adelphopoiesis* argument failed, the orientation argument beside the point, the Sodom demolition correct but irrelevant to Leviticus and Romans, and selective-literalism arguments to establish inconsistency rather than permission. His three options: (a) hold the ethic; (b) revise openly on stated grounds, as with slavery and usury — available to a serious believer and not the same as unbelief; (c) claim the text always meant something else, which he considers both implausible and strategically fragile, since it stakes a community's welcome of its own members on a philological argument that can be lost.

**Best case against him, which he states:** authorial intent is not obviously binding, and Christians have never read that way (Isaiah 7:14, Psalm 22, the Song of Songs); his reception-history criterion would have frozen slavery and women's subordination; the tradition's founding moves are revisionary — the Jerusalem Council overrode an explicit textual requirement on the evidence of the Spirit's work and reached for a scriptural warrant afterward; and his clean line between (b) and (c) may import a positivist theory of meaning a Spirit-guided community does not share.

## Part II — What the Six Actually Agree On

The convergences are more substantial than the disagreement, and several are concessions against interest.

1. **Sodom is not about this.** Both scholars agree Genesis 19 concerns gang rape and violated hospitality. The traditional analyst calls it "a proof-text traditionalists should never have leaned on."
2. **Leviticus 20:13's reciprocity is a real problem for the affirming reading.** Agent 1 lists it first among his weaknesses.
3. **Romans 1:26 includes women, and this defeats the pederasty-only reduction.** Conceded by all four who address it.
4. ***Arsenokoitai* probably derives from LXX Leviticus** — conceded by the affirming scholar and both analysts. But its actual referent is unattested, conceded by the traditional scholar.
5. **No ancient reader affirmed, and reception history is near-unanimous.** Nobody disputes this.
6. **Scripture never addresses a covenanted same-sex marriage as such.** The traditional scholar concedes this explicitly; his case runs on creation-order inference, "and inference is weaker than address."
7. **Every reader builds an interpretive bridge from an ancient text to a modern question.** Both analysts insist on this, from opposite conclusions.
8. **The church's treatment of gay people has been genuinely sinful**, conversion therapy is a lie that harms, and family rejection is gravely wrong. Both pastors, without qualification.
9. **Traditional churches have not paid the debts their own ethic incurs**, which is a large part of why it is experienced as unlivable. The traditional pastor argues this himself.
10. **Both pastors report the same fruit.** They disagree about what follows, but not about what they have observed.

What remains in dispute is therefore narrower than the volume of argument suggests. It is not *what the texts meant*. It is **what authority the meaning of an ancient prohibition has over a community that has never actually derived its ethics by originalism.**

## Part III — My Decision

The user's framing invited a middle answer: that it may be a sin and yet be blessed and accepted as a worldly action. I want to take that seriously, because it is close to something real — but I do not think it survives in the form stated, and I will say where I actually land.

### On the historical question, the traditionalists win

This should be said plainly, because affirming argument is often weakest exactly where it is loudest. The texts prohibit. The three strongest affirming exegetical moves — Sodom-was-hospitality, they-only-knew-pederasty, they-had-no-concept-of-orientation — are respectively true but irrelevant to Leviticus and Romans, defeated by Romans 1:26, and beside the point, since an act-based prohibition needs no theory of the desire behind it. Boswell's later work overreached. Chrysostom is a genuinely hard datum. Anyone who tells you the church simply misread these texts for two thousand years is overclaiming, and four of these six documents say so, including both affirming analysts.

The honest affirming achievement is narrower than the affirming case usually admits and more durable than it looks: the door is **not textually bolted**. Scripture never addresses the actual proposal. The traditional case must run through inference from creation order — which its own scholar concedes is weaker than address.

### But the historical question does not settle the normative one

Here the affirming side is right, and the traditional scholar's fifth conceded weakness is where his case comes apart. **No Christian community has ever derived its ethics by pure originalism, and this one least of all.** The Jerusalem Council overrode an explicit, perpetual, penalty-bearing command on the evidence of what the Spirit was manifestly doing, and located a scriptural warrant afterward. Peter's rooftop vision reversed a food law by revelation, not philology. Isaiah 56 overturns Deuteronomy 23 inside the canon. The pattern — encounter first, rereading second — is not a modern corruption of this tradition; it is one of its founding moves.

The Acts 15 counter-argument (the decree kept *porneia*) is a good one and I do not think it fails, but it proves less than it needs to. It shows the apostles retained a sexual ethic. It does not show that ethic's boundaries were thereafter unrevisable, since the same church went on to revise its readings of slavery, usury, lending, and — most damagingly — divorce.

**The divorce parallel is the sharpest argument in the entire collection**, and it is not a gotcha. By the traditionalists' own stated criterion — creation-grounded and reaffirmed across both testaments — the prohibition of remarriage after divorce is *better credentialed* than any text on same-sex relations: dominical, multiply attested in three gospels, independently confirmed by Paul who flags it as the Lord's command and not his own, and grounded by Jesus in the very Genesis passages traditionalists rely on here. It is nonetheless not enforced in most of the churches making the argument, and the exegetical relaxation followed the social change rather than preceding it. This is not an argument that two wrongs make a right. It is evidence about what the operative principle actually is — and it is not the principle being stated.

### Why the timing objection does not carry

It is the best traditionalist argument and it deserves a real answer rather than dismissal. Mine is that it proves too much, and that its proponents have already rejected it elsewhere. Abolition followed the same sequence: moral insight arrived from outside the text, and the reading changed afterward. The traditional analyst's distinction — that abolitionists argued *override* rather than reinterpretation — is acute, and I think it is correct as history. But it tells against a particular affirming *argumentative strategy*, not against the affirming conclusion. It is a reason to make the case differently, which is exactly what he recommends.

### Where I land

**I think a Christian community can bless a same-sex marriage and remain faithful — and that it must claim this as development, not as recovery.**

The grounds are the ones the tradition already uses: that celibacy is presented in Scripture as a charism given unevenly rather than an assignment issued categorically, and no one has warrant to assert its universal distribution across a class of people; that the goods Scripture asks marriage to bear — covenant, permanence, exclusivity, self-gift, sanctification through another person — are demonstrably present; that the fruit test cuts as it does; and that the tradition's own sorting principle, applied consistently, does not distinguish the revision it accepts from the one it refuses.

Three qualifications, all of which I mean:

**This is a judgment about which argument is stronger, not a ruling about anyone's faith.** The question is genuinely underdetermined, serious Christians land on both sides in good faith, and I hold the conclusion with less confidence than the volume of the debate would suggest is normal. If I am wrong, the traditional pastor's account of *why* I am wrong — that compassion is the thing most easily flattered — is the account I would expect to be true.

**The traditional pastor is right that the ethic has never actually been tried.** A church that provided household belonging, vowed friendship, leadership, end-of-life provision and protection from cruelty would be making a demand of a different character than the one gay Christians have actually received. I do not think this rescues the position, since after twenty centuries "we never implemented it properly" has diminishing force — but it means the fruit argument is weaker than affirming Christians typically treat it as being. Both pastors know this; only one of them says it.

**The asymmetry of error costs is a tiebreaker, not an argument.** If the traditionalists are wrong, the cost is measurable, present, and borne by identifiable people who did not choose their condition. If the affirming side is wrong, the cost is real but contested and deferred. Under genuine uncertainty that asymmetry properly counts for something. It does not count for much, and anyone who leads with it is substituting consequences for reasoning.

### On the middle position the user proposed

"A sin that can nonetheless be blessed" does not hold together as stated, because blessing is not neutral — to bless is to invoke God's favor on the thing blessed. A church saying "this is sin, and we bless it" is equivocating on one word or the other.

But there are three coherent positions in that neighborhood, and they are worth naming:

1. **Civil–ecclesial distinction.** A Christian may support same-sex *civil* marriage as a matter of public justice — equal legal protection, inheritance, medical decision-making, the standing to sit at a bedside — while holding that the church's sacramental marriage is something else. This is fully coherent, widely held, and probably the position of most traditionalists who have thought about it. It is close to what the user proposed and it survives scrutiny.
2. **Blessing persons rather than unions.** The 2023 Catholic declaration *Fiducia Supplicans* permitted non-liturgical blessings of people in irregular situations while withholding any blessing of the union as marriage. It is a real attempt at exactly this middle. It is worth noting that it satisfied almost no one on either side — which is itself evidence about the stability of the halfway house.
3. **Accommodation without endorsement.** Orthodoxy permits second marriages by *economia* — with a penitential rite, as concession rather than celebration. This is the most honest form of "permitted but not blessed," and it has the virtue of being an existing practice rather than an invention.

My view is that these positions are livable and considerably better than cruelty, but that they are unstable under pressure and tend to collapse toward one pole or the other — because the people in question notice the difference between being tolerated and being blessed, and a community cannot indefinitely hold that two things it does are only technically distinguishable.

### What is settled regardless

Whichever way a reader lands, the six documents converge on obligations that are not contingent on the outcome:

- Conversion therapy is ineffective and does harm. It should never be recommended, referred to, or defended.
- Family rejection of gay children is indefensible on any reading of Scripture, and the traditional pastor calls it a grave sin.
- Singling this out among sins, while a congregation quietly tolerates greed and serial divorce, is the specific hypocrisy Jesus addressed most sharply.
- A church holding the traditional line owes debts it has almost universally refused to pay. Holding the ethic while withholding the provision is not the traditional position; it is a failure to hold it.
- Nobody should be leading with contempt, and both pastors say so.

The disagreement is real and I do not want to dissolve it. But it is narrower than its participants usually behave as though it is, and a great deal of the harm has come from things both sides, argued at their best, condemn.
