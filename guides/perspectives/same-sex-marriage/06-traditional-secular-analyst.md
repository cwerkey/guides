# The Revision Is Real: A Secular Reading of the Affirming Hermeneutic

*Written by a non-believer with no stake in the outcome. I support same-sex civil marriage without reservation and think the moral question was settled long ago on grounds that have nothing to do with any scripture. My claim here is narrow and descriptive: as textual and historical analysis, the affirming reading of the Christian sources is a revision of the tradition, not a recovery of it — and the honest affirming move is to say so.*

## What I Am Not Arguing

I am not arguing that gay people should not marry. I am not arguing that the traditional sexual ethic is good, humane, or worth preserving; I think it has done considerable harm. I am not a religious believer and have no interest in policing anyone's doctrine.

I am arguing about a historical question that has been badly answered: what did these texts most likely mean, and how were they in fact understood? A great deal of affirming argument makes an empirical claim — that the texts never prohibited what they were long taken to prohibit, and that roughly two thousand years of readers got it wrong. That claim is assessable by ordinary scholarly means, and I think it is probably false. Nothing follows from that about how anyone should live. It follows only that a certain argumentative route is weaker than its users believe, and that a better route is available to them.

## 1. What the Texts Most Plausibly Meant

**Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13.** The Hebrew of 18:22 is odd and compressed — literally "with a male you shall not lie the lyings of a woman" — and the idiom *mishkevei ishah* is rare enough that its precise force is genuinely contested. This is the thinnest part of the evidence, and traditionalists who present it as transparent are overclaiming. Proposed rationales include procreative waste, boundary-violation in a system obsessed with mixing kinds, and status degradation of a male reduced to a receptive role.

The status-degradation reading is the one that would open an affirming path, since it would make the prohibition about humiliation rather than about the act. What weighs against it is 20:13's reciprocal liability: *both* parties are culpable. In a rule aimed at protecting a degraded or coerced party, that party is not normally condemned alongside his degrader. One can note that ancient law codes sometimes assign liability in ways that do not track modern notions of consent, and that is a fair caveat. But the plainest reading of the sentence is that the act itself, mutually performed, is prohibited.

**Romans 1:26–27.** The pederasty-only reduction — that Paul knew only exploitative, age-asymmetric, or slave-involving forms and condemned those — runs into the female clause. There was no institution of female pederasty in the Greco-Roman world for Paul to be condemning. Paul's structure is parallel: females exchange "natural use," and "likewise also" the males. To make 1:26 refer to something other than female-female eroticism, one must let the referent shift silently between the two halves of a deliberately paired construction. The reading is grammatically possible; it is not the economical one.

It is worth noting who established this most thoroughly. Bernadette Brooten's *Love Between Women* is the standard treatment, written by a scholar with no traditionalist sympathies, and it concludes that Paul is condemning female homoeroticism as such. That is the shape of the honest position I am describing: exegesis and ethics are separable, and a scholar can find that a text says something she rejects.

**1 Corinthians 6:9.** *Arsenokoitai* appears to be a coinage; Paul is its earliest known user. Its most probable derivation is from the Septuagint of Leviticus, where 18:22 and 20:13 place *arsenos* and *koitēn* in immediate proximity. This is not proof — compounds drift from their parts, and Dale Martin is right that later occurrences sometimes sit in lists of economic and exploitative sins. *Malakoi*, its companion term, is genuinely vague. But derivation plus pairing plus Paul's formation in a Hellenistic Jewish milieu — where Philo, Josephus, Pseudo-Phocylides and the Sibylline Oracles condemn male-male intercourse in general rather than pederastic terms — makes the Leviticus link the best available explanation.

Cumulatively: an act-based prohibition, restated in a Jewish context that read it broadly, restated again in Greek by a writer who includes women. Individually, each text has slack. Together, less.

## 2. Reception History

This is the part I regard as decisive for the descriptive question. From Second Temple Judaism through the rabbinic corpus, from the Greek and Latin fathers through the Syriac, Coptic, Armenian and Ethiopian churches, through Byzantium, the medieval West, every branch of the Reformation, and the modern global South — across languages, continents, political systems and twenty centuries — interpreters converge. Not perfectly, and not always for the same stated reasons, but overwhelmingly.

The single most awkward datum for the affirming philological case is John Chrysostom. He was a native Greek speaker reading Romans in his own language, close enough to classical antiquity that Greek pederasty was cultural memory rather than reconstruction, and he explicitly discusses the women in 1:26 and reads the passage as a general condemnation. If the affirming claim is that a linguistic and cultural nuance was lost, one has to explain why it was lost on the people who had the language and the culture.

The claim that everyone misread these texts until roughly 1955 is an extraordinary claim. It requires a uniform, culture-spanning, two-millennium error that happened to be corrected precisely when correcting it became socially convenient.

I will concede immediately what this argument does *not* establish. Near-universal reception was also wrong about slavery, geocentrism, and the subordination of women. Reception history tells you what a tradition taught. It cannot tell you what a tradition should now teach. That limitation is real, and I return to it below.

## 3. The Correlation Problem

Affirming exegesis appears after the mid-twentieth century, in Northwest Europe and North America, in communities where the surrounding culture had already begun to shift — and in that order. Acceptance first, then rereading.

The obvious objection is strong and I want to state it at full strength. Abolition and women's suffrage followed exactly this pattern. Moral insight arrived from outside the text, and the reading changed afterward. Social pressure can be the occasion on which a community finally notices something true, not merely the cause of its inventing something convenient. Genetic arguments are weak arguments; that a belief was produced by non-truth-tracking pressures does not make it false.

I still think the correlation is evidentially significant, for three reasons.

First, in the abolition case the strongest antislavery Christians largely did *not* claim the proslavery side had misread Paul. Many conceded the narrow textual argument and appealed instead to the Golden Rule, the image of God, and the trajectory of the whole. They argued for override, not for reinterpretation. That precedent supports option (b) below, not option (c).

Second, there is no comparable minority report. Slavery had dissenters — Gregory of Nyssa's homily on Ecclesiastes is a genuine pre-modern outlier — and usury had a live internal debate for centuries. There is essentially no premodern tradition of affirming same-sex unions to recover. A suppressed truth usually leaves traces.

Third, the affirming reading has traveled along lines of prior cultural acceptance rather than along lines of philological merit. Orthodox, Coptic, African Anglican, and Chinese communities read Greek and Hebrew too, and have no stake in an Anglo-American culture war; they have not been persuaded. Good philology usually travels better than that.

## 4. Where Affirming Scholarship Overreaches

**Boswell's *adelphopoiesis*.** The claim that Byzantine same-sex union rites were functionally marriages has not survived scholarly scrutiny. Specialists in the manuscripts read them as non-sexual kinship and alliance rituals — some containing prayers explicitly against carnality — often preserved in the same codices as canons penalizing male-male intercourse. Boswell was a serious historian and his 1980 book asked real questions; the 1994 argument overreached, and its continued popular circulation after the specialist verdict is itself a small piece of evidence about how this debate is conducted.

**"They had no concept of orientation."** Overstated as history — Aristophanes' speech in the *Symposium*, ancient medical writing on constitutional dispositions, and astrological texts all gesture at fixed erotic character — and, more importantly, beside the point. An act-based prohibition does not require a theory of the desire behind it. "Do not do X" is not silently restricted to people who do not want to do X. The incest prohibition does not presuppose a theory of attraction.

**Sodom.** The affirming side is right: Genesis 19 is about gang rape and violated hospitality, and Ezekiel 16:49 backs that up. This is a successful demolition of a proof-text traditionalists should never have leaned on. It does not touch Leviticus or Romans. It is frequently deployed as though it settled the matter.

**Selective literalism.** Shellfish and mixed fabrics establish that the tradition selects, and traditionalists owe an account of the principle of selection. But inconsistency is not permission. And the selection is not arbitrary on its own terms: the New Testament itself reworks food and purity law while the same documents restate the sexual prohibitions. One may reject that principle; one cannot pretend it isn't there.

## 5. Three Honest Options

**(a) Hold the traditional ethic.** Coherent, textually defensible, and in my view morally wrong — but the disagreement is about ethics, not about what the text says.

**(b) Revise openly.** Say: we now judge, on grounds of conscience, of what we have observed in the lives of gay Christians, and of a developing moral understanding, that the plain sense of these specific texts must yield — as it yielded on slavery, on usury, on lending at interest, and for most communions on divorce and the ordination of women. This is available to a serious believer and is not the same as unbelief. The tradition has a rich internal vocabulary for it: doctrinal development, the Spirit's continuing guidance, the distinction between letter and spirit, the conviction that the whole governs the parts. A community that takes this route is doing something recognizably Christian and something it has done before.

**(c) Claim the text always meant something else.** This requires an implausible empirical claim about the history of reading, and it is strategically fragile besides: it stakes a community's welcome of its own members on a philological argument that can be lost. If in ten years the consensus on *arsenokoitai* firms up against you, option (b) is undisturbed and option (c) collapses.

## The Best Case Against My Conclusion

The strongest reply does not contest my exegesis at all. It contests its relevance.

First: "what the author meant" is not obviously "what the tradition must now teach." Making authorial intent decisive is an originalist premise no tradition has actually practiced. Christians read Isaiah 7:14 christologically, Psalm 22 as passion narrative, the Song of Songs allegorically — none of which Paul's contemporaries would call recovering original meaning. To demand strict originalism here alone is itself selective.

Second: my reception-history argument proves too much. Applied consistently, it would have frozen slavery, geocentrism, women's subordination, and centuries of anti-Judaism in place. A criterion that would have been wrong every previous time it mattered is a poor criterion.

Third: Christianity's own internal logic is a logic of revising boundary markers. The Jerusalem Council overrode an explicit textual requirement — circumcision — on the evidence of what the Spirit was manifestly doing among Gentiles, and reached for a scriptural warrant only afterward. Jesus subordinates Sabbath rules to human need. Peter's rooftop vision reverses a food law by revelation, not by philology. Experience first, rereading second, is not a modern corruption of this tradition; it is one of its founding moves.

Fourth, and hardest for me: my sharp line between (b) and (c) may import a positivist theory of meaning the tradition does not share. In a community that believes a living Spirit guides its reading, "what it always meant" and "what it is now given to mean" may not be the clean alternatives a secular analyst assumes.

I grant the first three substantially — which is exactly why my conclusion is only descriptive, and why I think option (b) is not merely honest but strong. The fourth is the best point against me, and my reply is narrow: most affirming argument as actually conducted is made in historical-critical terms, claiming that Paul did not mean what he has been taken to mean. Those are terms on which it can be assessed, and on those terms I think it loses. A community that stopped making that claim would lose nothing it needs.

## Coda

The tradition says what it says. That is a fact about a set of old documents and their readers, not a verdict on anyone's marriage. My own verdict on the marriages is that they are fine, and that no ancient text has standing to say otherwise. What I am asking for is only that the revision be called a revision — which, if the tradition's own history is any guide, is the version most likely to hold.
