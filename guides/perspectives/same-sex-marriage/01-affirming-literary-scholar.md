# The Texts and What They Adjudicate: An Exegetical Brief

*Agent 1 — Affirming position, arguing as a literary and biblical scholar working from the Hebrew and Greek text, genre, rhetorical structure, and the historical setting of the producing communities.*

My claim is narrow, because the affirming case is frequently overstated by its friends. I do **not** argue that any biblical author approved of same-sex relationships; no such voice is attested in antiquity, Jewish or Christian. I argue something weaker: that the passages conventionally marshaled against same-sex marriage address questions other than the one now before the church, that their prohibitory force is bound to frames (cultic boundary, status hierarchy, idolatry-etiology, exploitative use) that do not straightforwardly transfer, and that the canon itself models revision of a scriptural bodily boundary. Where my reading is a minority one, I say so.

## 1. Genesis 1–2: Etiology, Not Taxonomy

Genesis 2:24 is marked, syntactically, as an etiology. The clause opens `עַל־כֵּן` (*'al-ken*, "therefore"), the standard Hebrew narrative formula introducing an explanation of an existing custom or name (cf. Gen 10:9; 11:9; 32:33). Westermann, Trible, and most of the commentary tradition read it as the narrator's aside, not as divine legislation. Its function is aetiological: it explains why the strange, powerful pull of sexual bonding exists, in terms of a primal unity now severed.

Two observations bear on prescriptivity. First, the verse has a man leave father and mother and cleave (`דָּבַק`, *davaq*, a covenant-loyalty verb) to his wife — inverting the patrilocal practice of Israelite society, where the woman left her household. It is not reporting social norms; it reaches for the *kinship* logic underneath them. "One flesh" (`בָּשָׂר אֶחָד`, *basar 'echad*) is kinship vocabulary before it is sexual vocabulary: Laban tells Jacob "you are my bone and my flesh" (Gen 29:14); Abimelech's kin are "your bone and your flesh" (Judg 9:2). The claim of 2:24 is that marriage creates kinship where none existed. That is precisely the claim same-sex couples make.

Second, Genesis 1:27's "male and female he created them" sits inside a chapter built almost entirely of merisms — heaven and earth, day and night, sea and dry land. Merisms name poles to encompass a whole; the text is not thereby denying dusk. Brownson and Vines press this; Gagnon replies, with force, that 1:28's procreative blessing anchors the pair concretely rather than merismatically. He is not wrong that the horizon of Genesis 1–2 is generative: this is a lineage-driven agrarian society for which fertility is survival. But that cuts as much for me as against. If the pairing is normative *because* it is generative, the norm concerns descendants — and the church has already declined to make fertility a condition of valid marriage.

The strongest textual purchase is `עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ` (*'ezer kenegdo*, 2:18), "a helper corresponding to him." *'Ezer* is not a subordinate term — it is used of God (Ps 121:2). The stated problem is aloneness; the stated remedy is a correspondent other. Sexual difference is how that remedy is instantiated in the narrative, not obviously what constitutes it.

## 2. Genesis 19 and Judges 19: What the Narratives Themselves Signal

Genesis 19 is a hospitality narrative set deliberately against Genesis 18. Abraham receives strangers with water, bread, and a calf; Sodom's men surround the house and demand to "know" (`יָדַע`) the guests — threatened gang rape of visitors, the standard ancient idiom for degrading an outsider. Lot's counter-offer of his daughters is unintelligible as a solution if the narrator's concern were male-male desire per se.

Judges 19 makes this near-certain. It is a near-verbatim retelling at Gibeah, and there the substitution is *accepted*: the Levite's concubine is raped through the night and dies. The narrator's verdict is not about the gender of the assailants' first target; it is that Israel has collapsed ("in those days there was no king in Israel," 19:1; 21:25). If Sodom's sin were homoeroticism, Gibeah's resolution would be no resolution at all.

Inner-biblical reception confirms this. Ezekiel 16:49–50 is diagnostic: "this was the guilt of your sister Sodom: pride, surfeit of food, and prosperous ease, but she did not aid the poor and needy; they were haughty and did *to'evah* before me." Jeremiah 23:14 names adultery, lying, and strengthening evildoers' hands. Wisdom of Solomon 19:13–17 makes the sin `μισοξενία`, hatred of strangers, comparing Sodom to Egypt. Sirach 16:8 says arrogance. No Hebrew Bible or Second Temple text prior to Philo (*De Abrahamo* 133–41) and Josephus reads Sodom as being about male-male intercourse.

Jude 7 is the real counter-instance and I take it seriously. But its phrase is `σαρκὸς ἑτέρας` — "other/different flesh," an odd way to describe same-sex activity, which involves *same* flesh. Jude 6 has just spoken of angels who left their proper dwelling, echoing the Watchers of 1 Enoch. The most economical reading is transgression of the human/angelic boundary — the men of Sodom sought messengers. That reading is not universal, and Gagnon contests it.

## 3. Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13: The Idiom and the Frame

Leviticus 18:22 reads `וְאֶת־זָכָר לֹא תִשְׁכַּב מִשְׁכְּבֵי אִשָּׁה` — "and with a male you shall not lie the lyings of a woman." The construct plural *mishkevei ishah* is rare: outside Lev 18:22 and 20:13 it appears only at Genesis 49:4 (Reuben "went up to your father's *mishkevei*"), denoting illicit violation of another man's sexual prerogative. The masculine idiom `מִשְׁכַּב זָכָר` (Num 31:17–18, 35; Judg 21:11–12) designates a woman who has been penetrated by a male. Olyan (*JHSex* 1994) and Walsh (*JBL* 2001) argue the idiom denotes specifically penetrative intercourse and that the original prohibition targeted the insertive partner, 20:13 later extending liability. A serious philological argument — and contested; Lipka and Gagnon read the idiom more broadly. I flag it as disputed, not settled.

The literary frame matters more than the philology. Leviticus 18 is bracketed by 18:1–5 and 18:24–30: do not do as Egypt or Canaan do; the land vomits out its inhabitants. This is boundary discourse — Israel constituted by unlikeness to its neighbors, with the land as sanction. Verse 21, immediately preceding, prohibits giving seed to Molech. The chapter interlaces cultic loyalty, lineage and seed, honor-shame status, and territorial holiness. Milgrom argued the prohibition is territorially and ethnically bounded and driven by seed-wastage; few follow him fully, but the frame he identifies is really there.

`תּוֹעֵבָה` (*to'evah*) is not a general moral term. It is used in Deuteronomy of idols (7:25–26), blemished sacrifice (17:1), cross-dressing (22:5), remarrying a divorced wife (24:4), and dishonest weights (25:16). It marks what is out of place against a boundary — sometimes gravely unjust, sometimes cultically improper. It is not the vocabulary the Decalogue uses for murder or theft. This does not make it trivial; it makes it categorically distinct from the natural-law reading later imposed on it.

Whether the concern is act-based, status-based, or cultic is genuinely open. My reading is that it is all three at once, and that the specifically *status* component — a male being made "as a woman," a degradation of honor in a patriarchal hierarchy — is doing substantial work. That component does not obviously survive translation into a context where the partners are peers.

## 4. Romans 1:18–32: Rhetorical Function

Romans 1:18–32 must be read to 2:1, where the trap springs: `διὸ ἀναπολόγητος εἶ, ὦ ἄνθρωπε πᾶς ὁ κρίνων` — "therefore you have no excuse, O man, every one of you who judges." The catalogue is a set-piece. Paul is reproducing a stock Hellenistic-Jewish indictment of Gentile idolatry (compare Wisdom of Solomon 13–14, which likewise runs idolatry → sexual disorder → vice list) in order to secure his auditor's assent and then indict the assenter. Stowers reads the passage as building a rhetorical persona; Campbell pushes this to full *prosopopoiia*, which I regard as overreach. But the rhetorical *function* — bait for 2:1 — is not seriously disputed.

The etiology matters. The subject is corporate humanity: they knew God, did not honor him, exchanged (`μετήλλαξαν`) his glory for images, and *therefore* God gave them over. Same-sex acts appear as a *consequence* of idolatry in a narrative of collective declension — not as anyone's individual biography, and not describing people who never made the exchange.

`παρὰ φύσιν` will not bear the weight placed on it. At Romans 11:24 God grafts the wild olive into the cultivated tree `παρὰ φύσιν` — contrary to the ordinary course, and salvific. At 1 Corinthians 11:14 `φύσις` teaches that long hair shames a man, a manifestly cultural judgment. Martin's point stands: *physis* in Paul denotes customary order, not metaphysical essence. The vocabulary of `χρῆσις` ("use," 1:26–27) and the excess language — `ἐξεκαύθησαν ἐν τῇ ὀρέξει`, "inflamed in their appetite" — belong to a Greco-Roman commonplace about insatiability, where the vice is uncontrolled desire overflowing its proper object.

## 5. 1 Corinthians 6:9 and 1 Timothy 1:10

`μαλακοί` means "soft." In Greek moral abuse it ranges over effeminacy, luxury, cowardice, self-indulgence; it is not a technical term for a sexual role, though it can carry that sense. `ἀρσενοκοῖται` is effectively a Pauline coinage, with no secure earlier attestation. The derivation argument is strong: LXX Leviticus 20:13 reads `μετὰ ἄρσενος οὐ κοιμηθήσῃ κοίτην γυναικός`, and *arsēn* + *koitē* plausibly crystallizes from it (Scroggs, Wright, Gagnon). I accept the derivation as probable.

What I deny is that derivation settles reference. Compounds routinely mean less or other than their parts. Martin's survey of later occurrences finds *arsenokoitēs* clustering in economic-exploitation contexts (Sibylline Oracles 2.70–77; Acts of John), and 1 Timothy 1:10 places it between `πόρνοις` and `ἀνδραποδισταῖς`, slave-traders — suggestive of a trafficking and coercion cluster. Only suggestive: vice lists associate loosely, and Nolland and Gagnon are right that argument from list-order is weak. Boswell's stronger claim, that the word means "male prostitute," has not survived scrutiny and I do not rely on it.

The honest conclusion: these terms condemn some form of male same-sex activity in a world where such activity was overwhelmingly pederastic, prostitutional, or master-slave. Whether they reach a covenanted partnership between peers is not answerable from the lexicon.

## 6. The Absence of Orientation

No Hebrew or Greek term in the canon corresponds to stable sexual orientation. Ancient sexual taxonomy organized by act, role, status, and excess: who penetrates whom, and whether appetite exceeds measure. Nissinen documents this thoroughly.

The absence blocks the inference that these texts have *adjudicated* a case they did not conceive: a prohibition framed around excessive appetite, status degradation, and idolatrous exchange does not automatically extend to a category its authors lacked. It does not show the authors would have approved. And the absence is not total — Aristophanes' speech in Plato's *Symposium*, pseudo-Aristotle's *Problemata* 4.26, and Hellenistic astrological texts gesture at dispositional accounts. Brooten presses this precisely against the affirming argument.

## 7. Canonical Precedent for Revising a Boundary

Three precedents, in ascending order of force.

**Isaiah 56:3–5** explicitly reverses Deuteronomy 23:1's exclusion of eunuchs, promising "a monument and a name better than sons and daughters." A bodily and reproductive exclusion in Torah is overturned within the canon, for people outside the procreative family structure. Acts 8:26–40 enacts it: "What prevents me from being baptized?" Matthew 19:12, immediately after Jesus cites Genesis, blesses those "eunuchs from birth" — the closest the Gospels come to acknowledging a non-standard given condition.

**Acts 10–15**: Peter is told "what God has made clean, do not call *koinon*" (10:15). At the Jerusalem council the decisive evidence is the Spirit's manifest work (15:8), with the Amos 9 citation supplied *after* (15:16–18) — Scripture reinterpreted in light of the Spirit's action, not the reverse. Luke Timothy Johnson's reading. Counterpoint: 15:20 retains `πορνεία`.

**Circumcision** is the strongest case. Genesis 17:14 makes it an everlasting covenant with *karet* for neglect. Paul relativizes it outright (Gal 5:2–6; Rom 2:25–29). If the church can do that to an unambiguous, perpetual, penalty-bearing bodily command, boundary-revision is a canonical capacity, not an innovation.

## Where My Reading Is Weakest

I will not pretend these are minor.

**Leviticus 20:13 is reciprocal and unqualified.** "Both of them have done *to'evah*; they shall surely be put to death; their blood is upon them." The status/penetration reading, which is the best philological tool I have, fits 18:22 far better than 20:13. Whatever 18:22's original scope, the Holiness Code as we have it holds both parties liable — which is exactly what an act-based, not merely status-based, prohibition looks like. Olyan's redactional explanation is possible but unproven.

**Romans 1:26 almost certainly includes female-female relations.** Miller's proposal that it means non-procreative heterosexual acts has not persuaded. Brooten's *Love Between Women* is decisive here, and Brooten is not an opponent of gay people — she is a careful historian whose findings damage the affirming case. Female-female relations in antiquity were not typically pederastic or master-slave; they do not fit the exploitation frame. Paul's objection therefore cannot be reduced to power asymmetry. It is more plausibly about gender-role inversion — which is a real objection, not a dodge, and one I can only answer by arguing that gender-role hierarchy is itself culturally bounded, an argument that goes beyond exegesis.

**Matthew 19:4–6.** Jesus cites Genesis 1:27 *and* 2:24 together in a halakhic argument, giving the male-female pairing normative weight in a legal ruling. My reply — that the question posed was divorce, not the range of valid marriage, and that the eunuch saying in 19:12 immediately complicates the picture — is a reply about scope, and scope arguments are weaker than direct ones.

**Reception history is unanimous.** Gagnon is right that every Jewish and Christian source in antiquity that addresses male-male intercourse condemns it, without exception, and that no ancient reader would have heard any of these texts as leaving room for same-sex marriage. My case does not claim otherwise; it claims the texts underdetermine a question they never framed. But an argument that no one heard for eighteen centuries carries a heavy burden, and I do not think exegesis alone discharges it. What exegesis can establish is that the door is not textually bolted. Whether to walk through it is an argument I have to hand to the other agents.
