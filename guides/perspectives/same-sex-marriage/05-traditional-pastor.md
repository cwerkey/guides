# The Shepherd's Burden: Holding the Historic Teaching Without Cruelty

*Agent 5 — Traditional / non-affirming position, written as a pastor with actual names and faces in view. My lane is pastoral theology and church practice; the lexical arguments over arsenokoitai and the Levitical background belong to Agent 4, and I take his work as read rather than repeating it.*

I want to begin by conceding the thing my side usually refuses to concede.

The church's treatment of gay people has been a sin. Not an unfortunate excess, not a matter of tone. A sin — requiring confession, restitution where possible, and a changed life. We handed our children to men who promised to make them straight and returned them broken. We told parents that throwing a seventeen-year-old out of the house was a form of love. We made this the one sin you could not confess without becoming a project. We let politicians rent our pulpits. I have sat with a man in his sixties who spent his twenties in an ex-gay program and cannot pray anymore, because every attempt returns him to that room.

I repudiate all of it. Conversion therapy is a lie and does documented harm. Family rejection of gay children is a grave sin against the fifth commandment's reciprocal duties. Treating this as the sin above sins, while our congregations quietly metabolize greed, gossip, and serial divorce, is the hypocrisy Jesus reserved his hottest language for.

And none of that changes what I believe Scripture requires of me. That is the hardest sentence I will write here, and I refuse to soften it by pretending the two halves cancel out.

## Why I Am Not Free

A pastor's authority is delegated and bounded. I did not write the covenant; I administer it. When a congregation calls me, they are not asking me to legislate — they are asking me to hand on what I received. The moment I begin revising terms because the present cost of keeping them has become visible to me, I have quietly relocated the authority from the text to my own compassion. My compassion is real. It is also the thing most easily flattered.

Jeremiah's indictment is of physicians, not enemies: "They have healed the wound of my people lightly, saying, 'Peace, peace,' when there is no peace." Those false prophets were not sadists. They were pastoral. They wanted the anxiety in the room to go down, and they had the words to make it go down. That is exactly my temptation at 11pm in a hospital corridor with a weeping mother, and I have felt its pull hard enough to know it is not hypothetical.

James 3:1 — "not many of you should become teachers, for you know that we who teach will be judged with greater strictness" — cuts in both directions, and my side almost never says the second half. Yes, I will answer for softening what God said. I will also answer for every burden I laid on a back that God never laid there, for every teenager who heard from me that he was disgusting, for confusing inherited cultural revulsion with the fear of the Lord. Both judgments are real, and the first does not license carelessness about the second. A man who is only afraid of being too soft has stopped reading the verse.

## The Cross Is Not a Metaphor

"If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow me." Christianity has always asked things of people that, considered on their own terms, are unbearable. It asks the woman whose husband left her at forty to remain unmarried while he is living. It asks the infertile couple to hold a hope that will not resolve. It asks the man in a marriage that went cold twenty years ago to stay and keep being kind. It asks the chronically single woman in her fifties to receive that absence as something other than a verdict on her worth. Jesus himself names a category of people who "have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom" and says plainly: "not everyone can receive this saying, but only those to whom it is given."

So celibacy is not an exotic cruelty invented for gay people. It is one instance of a demand the gospel makes broadly.

But the analogy limps, and gay Christians hear it deployed dishonestly all the time. The widow had her marriage. The infertile couple has each other. The straight single person is asked to wait, and waiting has a possible end. What I am asking of a nineteen-year-old is a closed door, at nineteen, forever, with no permitted hope this side of the resurrection. That is categorically heavier. Anyone on my side who says "we're all called to chastity" as though the burdens were symmetrical is not being truthful, and the hearer can tell.

## The Bill Comes to Us

If I hold this line, the church incurs debts. These are obligations, not sentiments, and the failure to pay them is the reason our ethic is widely regarded as unlivable — a judgment I think is largely correct as a description of how most traditional churches actually behave.

**Household belonging.** Wesley Hill and Eve Tushnet are right that the New Testament's primary family language is ecclesial, not nuclear: brothers, sisters, mothers, a household. If we mean it, gay members need keys to houses, standing invitations, a named place at Christmas nobody has to request, and someone whose job it is to notice when they are sick.

**A real theology of friendship.** We have let friendship decay into a leisure preference. Historic Christianity — Aelred of Rievaulx, the monastic traditions — treated it as a serious, vowable, permanent bond. If we forbid one form of committed intimacy, we are obligated to build the others in earnest, with public recognition and staying power.

**Leadership.** A celibate gay member should be able to teach, lead, preach, serve as an elder where their tradition permits. A church that says "you may stay but never lead" has communicated the actual doctrine, whatever it says out loud.

**Money and dying.** No spouse means no default next of kin, no second income, no one legally entitled to sit by the bed. The church must become the mechanism: housing costs, powers of attorney, hospital advocacy, executors, someone to organize the funeral. Marta — twenty years faithful to this, at real cost — once asked me who would decide if she had a stroke. The honest answer then was nobody. We fixed it for her specifically, which is an indictment, not an achievement.

**Protection.** Congregational cruelty is mine to police, not the gay member's. I once told a longtime deacon that one more joke would end his service. That is the job.

Most traditional churches have paid none of this. We demanded lifelong renunciation and offered a folding chair at the singles table. If the ethic is unlivable, it is substantially because we made it so.

## The Harm Question

I take the epidemiology seriously. Elevated rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidality among LGBT people, especially youth in religious families, are not activist invention. I have read the family-rejection literature and seen it in my own building.

My honest read is that the harm is concentrated in identifiable practices rather than distributed evenly across belief: rejection by parents, expulsion from home, shame-based formation, conversion therapy, the closet, the constant surveillance of one's own affections. Those are things churches do, and they can be stopped without changing doctrine. The data on affirming *family* environments, importantly, is stronger and cleaner than the data on affirming *theology* specifically.

But I will not pretend that distinction is as clean as I want it to be. When a fifteen-year-old hears "the thing you most want is permanently off-limits to you," a great deal of the harm may travel with the proposition itself and not merely with how gently I say it. I cannot fully separate my teaching from its reception, and the claim that I can may be self-serving. What I can say is that if my doctrine is true, the correct response to harm is not to make the doctrine false, but to eliminate every ounce of harm that is not intrinsic to it — and we are nowhere near having done that, so I do not yet know what the residue looks like.

## How I Would Actually Lead

**Teaching sequence.** Never a standalone series, never in the first year, never in an election season. It comes inside a long treatment of the body, singleness, friendship, marriage, and desire, in which straight people are asked to give something up before gay people are.

**Membership.** I would receive into membership, baptize, and commune a married same-sex couple. Baptism is God's claim on a person, not my certificate of their tidiness. The Table is for sinners, and I do not fence it against this while leaving it open to the landlord gouging his tenants. I would be honest with them from the start about where I land and where the church's teaching and leadership offices sit.

**Leadership.** This is where I would hold a line, and where I lose people. I would not ordain or install into eldership someone in a same-sex marriage — as I would not install someone in a marriage I believed to be adulterous. I know how that lands. I have not found a way to make it land better without lying.

**Weddings.** I would not officiate. I told Ruth, whose daughter asked, exactly that — and then asked whether I could sit in the back, because I loved her daughter and wanted to be there. She said yes. I went. Some colleagues call that cowardice; some of her family found my presence insulting. I would do it again.

**Disagreement.** Members may disagree with me openly and remain members in full standing. I am not running a purity operation.

**Parents.** My first sentence is always the same: do not lose your child. Whatever you believe, you have one job right now, and it is to make sure they know the door is open. A parent who chooses doctrinal clarity over their kid has understood neither.

**Refusals.** I will not refer anyone to conversion therapy, out anyone, let this become a political litmus test, or preach a sermon whose energy comes from contempt.

## Two Thousand Years

For twenty centuries, across Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Ethiopian, Slavic, and now African and Asian Christianity — in cultures with wildly different sexual mores, including several relaxed about male same-sex behavior — the church has taught the same thing here. The affirming position is concentrated in a few wealthy Western denominations over roughly sixty years. That is not a knockdown argument; consensus was wrong about slavery and about women, and those cases keep me honest. But it allocates the burden of proof. An innovation this recent and this narrow must explain not only the texts but why the Spirit withheld this from Augustine, Aquinas, Wesley, and the entire global South.

## What Keeps Me Up At Night

**I am asking of others a cost I do not bear.** I go home to my wife. I have never been told that the deepest longing of my life is permanently disallowed. There is something structurally suspect about a married man defending lifelong celibacy for people whose situation he will never occupy, and my ability to construct arguments does not dissolve that suspicion.

**The fruit is often despair.** Jesus said you would know a tree by its fruit. When I look at what my teaching has actually produced in the gay people I have known — not what it produces in theory — I see more exhaustion, more hiddenness, more people quietly leaving the faith than flourishing celibacy. Marta is real. So are the eleven others who are gone. That arithmetic will not come out the way I want.

**The church has been wrong before, and insisted it wasn't.** We were certain about slavery, about usury, about the status of women, and in each case we had texts. The people defending the consensus sounded exactly like me — the plain sense, the historic teaching, the danger of accommodation. I do not know, from the inside, how to be sure I am not that.

**"We love you but you may never have a partner" may be indistinguishable from rejection.** Not logically — I can draw the distinction on a whiteboard. But to the person receiving it, over a lifetime, that distinction may simply not exist. If the received meaning is the real meaning, my gentleness is only a way of feeling better about the same wound.

**I may be defending an institution rather than the truth.** I have a job, a tradition, a network, colleagues who would stop returning my calls. Changing my mind is expensive for me in ways that have nothing to do with faithfulness. I would like to believe I have accounted for that. I am not confident that I have.

I hold this teaching because I believe it is what God has said, and I am not free to improve on it. I hold it with my hands shaking. If I am wrong, I have harmed people I love, and I will answer for it.
