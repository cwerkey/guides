# The Coherence Test: A Secular Analysis of the Affirming Position

*Written from outside the faith. I do not hold that the Bible is divinely inspired, but I take it seriously as a set of historical documents and I take believers' reasoning seriously on its own terms. The question here is not whether Christianity is true; it is which of two internal positions is more coherent given the interpretive commitments Christians actually hold. My judgment is that the affirming position is.*

## 1. What Kind of Object the Text Is

The material at issue was produced across roughly a millennium by authors who did not know one another, in at least three languages, under Egyptian, Assyrian, Babylonian, Persian, Hellenistic, and Roman political conditions. Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 are widely held to be distinct compositions with distinct vocabularies and cosmologies. Leviticus 18 and 20 belong to the Holiness Code, a priestly boundary-drawing document whose organizing anxiety is the distinction between Israel and the Egyptians and Canaanites named at its head. Romans is an occasional letter to a mixed congregation Paul had not yet met. The Pastorals are, on most critical accounts, later and pseudonymous.

Three social systems matter for reading any of it. First, honor and shame: sexual conduct was evaluated less by consent or affection than by whether it preserved the standing of a household. Second, the patriarchal household as an economic unit: marriage transferred women between male-headed estates and secured lineage and land. Third, purity as boundary maintenance, in Mary Douglas's sense — proscribed mixtures often mark category violations rather than harms.

Roman sexual ethics ran along a different axis than ours. Status and penetrative role, not the sex of the partner, organized the moral field; the shame attached to the man who was penetrated, and a free adult male's use of slaves or non-citizens drew little censure. Leviticus 18:22's construction — a male lying with a male "as with a woman" — sits legibly inside that grid: a man made into a woman, a category breach.

None of this dissolves the texts. Leviticus prohibits, unambiguously. Paul in Romans 1 lists same-sex conduct as an instance of humanity's disordering, and *arsenokoitēs* in 1 Corinthians 6 is most plausibly a coinage from the Septuagint of Leviticus 20:13 — a point that genuinely favors traditionalists, since it ties Paul's prohibition to Israel's law rather than to Greco-Roman pederasty alone. Read flatly, the texts prohibit.

What follows is narrower but decisive: nothing transfers from that world to a modern question without a bridge. Every reader must say which features of an ancient prohibition were essential and which incidental. Traditionalists build such a bridge; so do affirming readers. The dispute is not text versus culture. It is whose bridge has fewer unsupported spans.

## 2. The Selective-Literalism Audit

The audit is often run badly. "You eat shellfish" is a weak move, and I will not make it: the New Testament explicitly releases dietary law (Mark 7, Acts 10 and 15) and relativizes Sabbath (Colossians 2). Traditionalists have a textual warrant there, and the gotcha fails.

The serious cases are elsewhere.

**Divorce and remarriage.** This is the strongest consistency point available. Prohibition of remarriage after divorce is attributed to Jesus in Mark, Matthew, and Luke, and independently by Paul in 1 Corinthians 7, where he flags it as the Lord's command rather than his own. By the criteria traditionalists themselves use — dominical, multiply attested, grounded in the Genesis creation account — it is better credentialed than any text on same-sex conduct. Yet nearly every Protestant body now permits remarriage, having expanded the Matthean *porneia* exception and the Pauline privilege into a broad pastoral discretion. That expansion happened after divorce became common among members.

**Usury.** Prohibited in Deuteronomy, Psalms, Ezekiel, and Luke 6; treated as grave sin through the medieval period; reinterpreted after the Reformation via a productive-capital distinction the texts never draw. Economic change moved first; the exegesis followed and was then narrated as development.

**Slavery.** In the American debate the literalists had the better prooftexts, as Mark Noll has documented. Abolition required exactly the move traditionalists now reject: a trajectory reading, in which the text's direction rather than its statements governs. The tradition has already accepted a case where the plain reading lost.

**Head coverings and women's silence.** 1 Timothy 2 grounds its restriction in creation order — Adam formed first — the same argumentative form used for marriage. Veiling is nonetheless gone almost everywhere, and most evangelical practice has relaxed on women teaching. Creation-order grounding did not, in the event, preserve the practice.

Traditionalists answer with three sorting principles. The moral/ceremonial/civil division is the weakest: it is a later systematization, and Leviticus 18 interleaves categories without marking them, though it is not arbitrary, since the New Testament does sort. Creation-order argument from Genesis, reaffirmed by Jesus in Mark 10, is much stronger — and notably independent of Leviticus. Its limits are that the Mark 10 citation functions to bind spouses in a divorce dispute rather than to enumerate permissible pairings, that the same logic in 1 Timothy 2 was abandoned, and that a strict design-teleology reading is already qualified by the New Testament's own high valuation of celibacy. Sustained New Testament reaffirmation is the best answer of the three, and it defeats the ceremonial-lapse argument cleanly.

So traditionalists do possess a principle: retain what is grounded in creation and reaffirmed across both testaments. The audit's actual finding is not that they lack a rule. It is that divorce and remarriage satisfies that rule at least as well and is not enforced. The inconsistency is real, and it lies in application.

## 3. What the Evidence Shows

Both sides make empirical claims, and those claims are checkable even though they cannot settle a normative question on their own.

**Orientation change.** There is no credible evidence that reparative or conversion therapy changes sexual orientation. The one prominent study claiming otherwise was retracted by its author in 2012; the most methodologically careful sympathetic study found modest self-reported shifts with high attrition and heavy reliance on self-report. Observational studies associate exposure with elevated suicidality, though selection effects are severe — the more distressed and more devout are likelier to enter — so the harm finding should be stated as plausible rather than established. The absence-of-efficacy finding is the robust one.

**Rejection versus acceptance.** Family and congregational rejection is consistently associated with substantially worse mental health outcomes, including suicide attempts. Religiosity, ordinarily protective in general populations, loses that protective association and sometimes reverses for LGB people in non-affirming contexts. These are observational, some from small samples, and confounded — but the direction is stable across designs.

**Children.** The mainstream finding of no significant differences in psychological adjustment has survived the most-cited challenge, whose misclassification problems were widely documented, and is supported by more recent population-register work on educational outcomes. Selection effects run in both directions.

**Stability.** Dissolution rates are broadly comparable to different-sex marriages; Scandinavian registry data suggests somewhat higher dissolution among female couples and lower among male couples. The time series is short.

What this evidence can do is limited but not nothing. It cannot establish that a practice is licit. It can defeat a specific empirical premise that traditionalists themselves introduced — that these unions are intrinsically damaging and that the alternative is available. On that narrow question, the evidence is against them.

## 4. The Category Problem, Correctly Weighted

The claim that ancient writers had no concept of sexual orientation is usually overstated. Greco-Roman sources contain persistent-preference vocabulary and etiologies: the myth in Plato's *Symposium*, astrological texts assigning lifelong dispositions, medical writers on constitutionally "soft" men. Bernadette Brooten's work shows first-century awareness of something orientation-like for women. The strong version of the argument fails.

More importantly, it proves too little. A prohibition stated over acts — no male shall lie with a male — requires no theory of disposition to be intended universally. Laws against theft do not presuppose a taxonomy of kleptomania. So the orientation point cannot carry weight on its own.

It does real work only in combination with a claim about act-individuation: that what the ancient texts proscribe, under the description that made them proscribe it — status degradation, excess, idolatrous cult, the unmaking of a man — is not the same act-type as a covenanted union between social equals. That is a live and serious argument. But it is an argument about how acts are described, not about orientation, and affirming writers weaken their case when they conflate the two.

## 5. Why I Judge the Affirming Reading Better Supported

**Auxiliary assumptions.** The traditionalist package requires: a tripartite division of law the text does not mark; that *physis* in Romans 1 means designed teleology, despite Paul using the same word for God acting contrary to nature in Romans 11 and for hair length in 1 Corinthians 11; that creation-order grounding is decisive here but was properly set aside in 1 Timothy 2; and that dominical divorce teaching admits pastoral development while these texts do not. The affirming package requires: a trajectory hermeneutic already licensed by the slavery case; an act-individuation claim; and a governing principle — covenant fidelity, neighbor-love, judgment by fruits — already operative elsewhere. Fewer spans, and each already load-bearing in accepted cases.

**Self-description.** Affirming Christians generally concede that they are doing developmental work and name the principle doing it. Traditionalists frequently describe themselves as simply reading the text while exercising the same selectivity. That gap between method and self-account is a coherence cost, and it is a claim about description, not sincerity.

**The revision pattern.** Slavery, usury, heliocentrism, interracial marriage, women's ordination: the recurring shape is external pressure, then resistance, then re-grounding in the tradition's own resources, then retrospective description as internal development. This does not show that any given revision is correct — the same institutions have also resisted pressure in ways later vindicated. It shows only that revision is not per se infidelity, which removes the traditionalist's structural objection while leaving the substantive one intact.

The affirming position wins, in my judgment, not because the ancient texts favor it — read flatly, they do not — but because the interpretive method these communities already use, applied consistently, delivers it.

## The Best Case Against My Conclusion

A serious traditionalist analyst would respond roughly as follows, and the response has force.

First, timing. No affirming reading of these texts existed before the mid-twentieth century, and it emerged precisely where and when the surrounding culture moved, among the demographics most exposed to that movement. A hermeneutic that reliably outputs elite consensus is better explained by the consensus than by the exegesis. That is a Bayesian point, not an ad hominem, and it is the strongest single argument on the traditionalist side.

Second, distinctiveness. A tradition whose ethics track the culture with a two-decade lag has no independent moral content and forfeits standing to criticize anything. Its claim to speak from outside becomes decorative.

Third, my audit cuts both ways. Inconsistency can be cured by tightening rather than loosening, and the demand that lax remarriage practice justify a further revision is a request to compound an error. Moreover, the bodies with the most disciplined marriage practice — Catholic annulment discipline, Orthodox penitential remarriage — are largely immune to the charge. My audit lands hard on a particular strand of evangelical practice, not on traditionalism as such.

Fourth, outcome data cannot settle normative questions. Christianity has never held that a practice is licit because its practitioners flourish; celibacy, martyrdom, and enforced monogamy in polygamous societies all impose costs the tradition accepted as intelligible. Demonstrating that a prohibition is painful is not evidence that it is false.

Fifth, and most pointed: the honest secular reading of the texts is that they prohibit. My own section one concedes it. The cleanest affirming position is therefore "we have learned something our texts did not know, and we are choosing it" — which most affirming Christians will not say, because saying it would cost them the authority they are trying to claim. At least, the traditionalist may say, we are not pretending.

I accept most of this and do not think it overturns the conclusion. Parsimony in auxiliary assumptions may not be the right criterion for a tradition that also weighs consensus and magisterium, and I have given two millennia of near-unanimity less weight than an insider would. But the timing objection applies with equal force to abolition, and the pretense objection applies with equal force to every retroactive re-grounding the tradition now regards as growth. Applied consistently, the traditionalist's own tests do not discriminate between the revision they accept and the one they refuse. That is why, from outside, the affirming reading looks like the more defensible one.
