# The Grammar of Creation: A Textual Case Against Same-Sex Marriage

*Agent 4 — Traditional / Non-Affirming position, argued as a literary and biblical scholar. My warrant is the text: Hebrew and Greek lexis, genre, rhetorical structure, and the ancient communities that first heard these words. I make no appeal to disgust, to contemporary politics, or to consequences. The people whose lives are at stake in this question are made in the image of God and are owed better than caricature; I intend to give them argument instead.*

## 1. Genesis 1–2: Sexual Difference Inside the Image

Genesis 1:27 is a tricolon, and its third line is the surprise. Two lines establish the *imago Dei* ("in the image of God he created him"), and then, without transition, comes *zākār ûneqēbâ bārāʾ ʾōtām* — "male and female he created them." The plural pronoun arrives with the sexed pair. Whatever else the image means in the Priestly writer's theology, the text places sexual difference inside the sentence that defines humanity, not in an appendix to it. The blessing of 1:28 then presupposes that difference.

Genesis 2 is a different genre — narrative etiology rather than liturgical cosmogony — but it converges. The problem is stated as *lōʾ-ṭôb*, "not good," and the solution is an *ʿēzer kenegdô*, a helper *corresponding to* him: *neged* connotes counterpart, something set opposite. The animals are paraded and fail the test (2:20), which makes the point that the deficiency is not loneliness in general but the absence of a like-yet-other. The narrator's comment in 2:24 — a man leaves father and mother, *dābaq* ("cleaves") to his wife, and they become *bāśār ʾeḥād* — reads most naturally as reunion of what 2:21–22 divided.

Affirming readers, following the argument that "one flesh" is kinship language (as in Genesis 29:14, "you are my bone and my flesh"), press that *bāśār ʾeḥād* denotes a new kin-bond, not anatomical complementarity. That is a fair lexical observation and I accept part of it: *one flesh* certainly includes kinship. But the immediate context supplies the differentiating detail — this kin-bond is constituted by the division and rejoining of an original *ʾādām* into *ʾîš* and *ʾiššâ*, a wordplay the narrator makes explicit in 2:23. Kinship in general does not need that etiology; this one does.

Does the narrative function normatively or merely explain a custom? The canon answers. Malachi 2:14–15 invokes creation against faithless divorce; the Damascus Document (CD 4:20–21) cites Genesis 1:27 — "male and female he created them" — as *the principle of creation* against polygamy; and Jesus does the same against divorce. Within Second Temple Judaism, Genesis 1–2 was already functioning as marital *halakhah*, not as folklore.

## 2. Matthew 19:3–6 // Mark 10:6–9: The Shape of the Answer

Jesus is asked a divorce question. He answers with two citations: Genesis 1:27 (*arsen kai thēly epoiēsen autous*) and Genesis 2:24. This is the pivot of my case, and I want to state both its force and its limit.

The objection is real and I concede it plainly: Jesus is not addressing same-sex marriage. Nobody asked him. Any argument from this passage is an argument from the *shape* of an answer given to another question.

But the shape is not nothing. Genesis 2:24 alone answers the divorce question — "the two shall become one flesh… what God has joined, let no one separate" delivers permanence without help. The male-female citation is logically surplus to the divorce ruling, and Jesus supplies it anyway, prefacing it with *ap' archēs ktiseōs* ("from the beginning of creation," Mark 10:6). He grounds the indissolubility of the pair in the createdness of the pair. In the Markan sequence, 1:27 is premise and 2:24 is conclusion. A definition of marriage whose premise is sexual difference does not obviously extend to unions lacking it. This is inference, not statement — but it is inference from what a teacher chose to say when he did not have to.

## 3. Leviticus 18:22; 20:13: Reading the Holiness Code as a Code

Leviticus 18:22 reads *weʾet-zākār lōʾ tiškab miškebê ʾiššâ* — "with a male you shall not lie the lyings of a woman" — and 20:13 restates it with a penalty. Three structural observations.

First, the literary neighborhood. These verses sit in a catalogue of sexual prohibitions: incest (18:6–18), menstrual intercourse, adultery (18:20), Molech offering (18:21), bestiality (18:23). Nearly every reader across the spectrum still treats incest, adultery, and bestiality as trans-cultural. A hermeneutic that dissolves v. 22 as cultic or purity-bound has to explain why it does not dissolve its neighbors, and the usual answer — consent and harm — is a criterion imported from outside the text, not derived from the Code's own logic, which is creational and genealogical.

Second, the reciprocity of 20:13. Both parties are liable: *šenêhem* … *môt yûmātû*. This tells against the strongest revisionist readings. If the offense were the humiliation of a passive male — a status or penetration ethic, as in much Greco-Roman thought — the penetrated party would not be equally culpable; he would be the victim. If it were cultic prostitution, we would expect the *qādēš* vocabulary the Deuteronomist actually uses (Deut 23:17–18) and which is conspicuously absent here. The reciprocal form points to the act itself, not to the roles or the shrine.

Third, *tôʿēbâ*. I will not overclaim it. *Tôʿēbâ* is used of dietary matters (Deut 14:3), which affirming readers rightly note. But it is also used of dishonest weights (Deut 25:16), of the injustices of Proverbs 6:16–19, and of idolatry. It is a boundary-marking word, not a technical term for ritual impurity; it does not by itself carry either side's case, and readers on both sides should stop treating it as a trump.

## 4. Romans 1:24–27: The Load-Bearing Text

Paul's paragraph is built on Genesis. Romans 1:20 speaks of *apo ktiseōs kosmou*; 1:23 alludes to the LXX vocabulary of Genesis 1's created orders (*homoiōmati eikonos* … birds, quadrupeds, reptiles); and then in 1:26–27 Paul abandons the Hellenistic euphemisms available to him and writes *thēleiai* and *arsenes* — the LXX words of Genesis 1:27 — rather than *gynaikes* and *andres*. Hays and Gagnon are right that this diction is not accidental. Paul is narrating a movement: exchange of the Creator (1:25) is mirrored by exchange of created sexual difference (1:26–27). The threefold *metēllaxan* / *paredōken* structure is the argument.

*Para physin.* The honest concession first: Paul uses *para physin* in Romans 11:24 of God grafting a wild olive branch, where the sense is plainly not moral censure. So the phrase is not self-interpreting. But in 1:26 it is glossed by its own context — *physikēn chrēsin* is defined by *thēleias* in v. 27, and the whole runs on the creation vocabulary of 1:20–25. Paul's "nature" here is not a Stoic commonplace about convention; it is Genesis read as an order.

The decisive detail is v. 26. Paul includes female-female relations. This is the single hardest fact for the reduction of the passage to pederasty or master–slave exploitation, since neither of those structures characterized Roman-era female homoeroticism. Bernadette Brooten's *Love Between Women* (1996) — a work written from an affirming standpoint and all the more valuable for it — documents that female-female relations were known, discussed, satirized, and sometimes formalized. Brooten's own conclusion is that Paul condemned them; her critique of Paul is that his grounds were a gender hierarchy she rejects. That is a coherent position, but it is not the claim that Paul only knew exploitation.

Does the rhetorical trajectory toward 2:1 ("therefore you are without excuse, whoever you are who judge") void the moral content of 1:26–27? Stanley Stowers and others read 1:18–32 as a rhetorical trap. I accept the rhetoric — Paul is setting up the moralizing reader. But a trap works only if its premises are shared: the "sting" of 2:1 is *you do the same things*, which presupposes the vice list is genuinely vicious. Rhetoric of this shape borrows the audience's agreement; it does not retract it.

## 5. 1 Corinthians 6:9–11 and 1 Timothy 1:10: *Arsenokoitai*

*Arsenokoitēs* has no attested pre-Pauline usage; Paul (or his circle) appears to have coined it. The best account of the coinage is the LXX of Leviticus 20:13: *hos an koimēthē meta **arsenos** **koitēn** gynaikos*. The two morphemes stand adjacent in that verse, and Paul's letters elsewhere show heavy dependence on Leviticus 18–20 for sexual vocabulary. In 1 Corinthians 6:9 it is paired with *malakoi* ("soft"), plausibly the receptive partner to *arsenokoitai*'s active — a distinction the Greco-Roman world made constantly.

The concessions here are substantial and I make them without hedging. The referent of *arsenokoitai* is genuinely unattested; the *malakos*/*arsenokoitēs* pairing as passive/active is an inference, and *malakos* elsewhere means effeminate, self-indulgent, or morally soft without sexual reference. Compound words do not always mean the sum of their parts. Dale Martin's caution is methodologically sound. What I claim is comparative, not certain: etymology plus the LXX adjacency plus Paul's demonstrable Leviticus dependence plus the vice-list's Decalogue framing in 1 Timothy 1:9–10 constitutes the strongest available account, and the alternatives (male prostitution, economic exploitation) have less textual purchase, not more.

## 6. The Second Temple Frame — and What the Greeks Actually Knew

Hellenistic Judaism speaks with a striking uniformity here: Philo (*De Abrahamo* 135–137; *Spec. Leg.* 3.37–42), Josephus (*Against Apion* 2.199), *Sibylline Oracles* 3.596–600, Pseudo-Phocylides 190–192, and the creational marital halakhah of CD 4:20–21. William Loader — who has surveyed this literature more exhaustively than anyone and who personally favors change — concludes that the Jewish sources are consistently negative and that Paul stands within them. That a scholar with no traditionalist stake reaches this conclusion should carry weight.

The crucial test is whether the surrounding culture knew only exploitative forms. It did not, and this must be conceded to be tested honestly. Plato's *Symposium* (Aristophanes' speech) imagines lifelong same-sex pairing; Roman satire mocks men who took male "wives" in ceremonies, and Nero's marriages are reported by Tacitus and Suetonius; Brooten's magical binding spells attest women seeking enduring bonds with women. John Boswell went further, arguing that *adelphopoiesis* rites were same-sex unions — a reading I think fails (the liturgies use kinship, not nuptial, formulae, and Shaw and others have shown the texts will not bear the load), but Boswell's larger point stands: the ancients had more categories than the "pederasty only" thesis allows. Martti Nissinen is right that no ancient writer had a modern concept of sexual orientation. What follows, though, is that Paul's prohibition is framed by *act and created structure*, not by a theory of desire he did not hold — which makes it harder, not easier, to confine to exploitation.

## 7. Acts 15 and the Trajectory Argument

The strongest affirming hermeneutic is not exegetical but canonical: the church admitted Gentiles uncircumcised, so it can revise here. The disanalogy is in the text of the decree itself. Acts 15:20, 29 relaxes circumcision and retains four items — idol-food, blood, strangled meat, and *porneia* — which map onto the resident-alien legislation of Leviticus 17–18, the very chapters containing 18:22. The Jerusalem Council's precedent is precisely that boundary markers of ethnic identity fall while the sexual ethic of Leviticus 18 is carried into Gentile Christianity. Acts 15 is not a warrant for revision; on the sexual question it is the counter-example.

## Where My Reading Is Weakest

1. **Leviticus keeps bad company for my case.** Chapter 18's prohibitions stand a few verses from menstrual-purity law and a few chapters from mixed fabrics and shellfish. I have argued a structural distinction, but the Code itself does not label which prohibitions are trans-cultural. That sorting is done by later Christian judgment, and my opponents are entitled to ask by what criterion.

2. **The referent of *arsenokoitai* is genuinely unknown.** I have offered the most probable derivation, not a demonstrated one. If the LXX derivation fails, 1 Corinthians 6:9 and 1 Timothy 1:10 largely drop out of the case, leaving Romans 1 and creation.

3. **Romans 1 is embedded in a rhetorical trap.** Paul's target is the judging reader, and any traditionalist who quotes 1:26–27 without landing on 2:1 has misread the paragraph. The passage indicts my own use of it.

4. **Scripture nowhere addresses a covenanted, monogamous, mutual same-sex marriage.** Every text I have cited addresses acts within frameworks foreign to that proposal. My argument must therefore run through creation-order inference, and inference is weaker than address.

5. **The church has revised readings before while denying it was revising** — on slavery, on usury, on Galileo, on divorce and remarriage in practice. The confidence that "the text is plain" has a poor track record. That is not an argument that I am wrong; it is a reason to hold this reading with less certainty than its defenders usually display.

I take the texts to point one direction with real consistency. I do not take that consistency to be self-evidently the end of the conversation.
