# The Pastor's Case: Marriage Discipline as Faithful Care

*Agent 2 — Affirming. Written from the chair of a pastor with four hundred members, nine elders, a credential I could lose, and a list of names I carry into prayer every Thursday morning.*

I am not arguing that Scripture is negotiable, that the church should follow the culture, or that love is self-justifying. I hold the sixty-six books as God's word, binding on us. Agent 1 has made the exegetical case on the disputed texts; I will not rehearse it. My burden is harder: that when we ask what the whole counsel of Scripture teaches about marriage, celibacy, sanctification, and the testing of doctrine by its fruit, a marriage discipline that includes covenanted same-sex couples is more faithful than the one I inherited. Not kinder. More faithful.

## I. The Pastoral Data I Cannot Un-See

Twenty-two years of ministry produce a file drawer.

There is a man I will call Daniel, who at thirty-one had done everything the traditional teaching asked: reparative counseling, the prayers, marriage to a godly woman in obedience, two children. He sat in my office and told me he had not been able to pray in four years. Not would not — could not. "I open my mouth and nothing comes out, because the first thing out would be the thing I'm not allowed to say." His wife learned the truth from someone else. That marriage ended, two children lost a home, and I had counseled him into it.

There is a woman I will call Ruth, seventy-three, who spent her life among us as "a single lady," and who told me at the funeral of the woman she had lived with for forty-one years that no one had ever prayed over her as a wife. And there is the seventeen-year-old — there is always a seventeen-year-old — who came out to me before his parents because he had done the math on what the youth group would say.

I am not arguing from clinical statistics but from Matthew 7. Jesus gives us a test: *you will know them by their fruits; a good tree cannot bear bad fruit*. It deserves honest application in both directions.

Against the traditional teaching, the fruit I have watched at close range: closeted despair, mixed-orientation marriages built on a sincere lie, ruptured families, a generation for whom the church's first word was a word about their disqualification. Against my own position: division, and weakened confidence in Scripture among people who conclude that if this text bent, all texts bend.

But the fruit test is not a popularity test. It asks what a teaching *does in a life over time*. What I have watched grow in the couples I now bless is the fruit of Galatians 5: patience, kindness, faithfulness, self-control — self-control especially, the thing the traditional teaching promises and, in my observation, most often fails to deliver.

## II. What Marriage Is, and Whether It Is Present

Marriage in Scripture is covenant, not contract: exclusive, permanent, publicly witnessed, God-attested (Malachi 2:14). It is a school of sanctification in which two sinners are welded to someone who will not go away and are thereby confronted, humbled, and slowly conformed to Christ. And in Ephesians 5 Paul claims that this covenant *signifies* Christ's self-donating love for the church.

Notice what Paul asks marriage to bear there: submit to one another out of reverence for Christ; love as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her; nourish and cherish. The load-bearing content is self-gift, permanence, covenant fidelity — not procreation, which Paul does not mention here, and not a symbolic dependence on anatomical difference, which is more often read into the passage than out of it.

Two women in my congregation have been together nineteen years. One nursed the other through a cancer that should have killed her — the shaved head, the ostomy bag, the year without dignity. That is *gave himself up for her*. I watched it, and I will not stand in my pulpit and tell my people that what they saw was not what Paul described.

And here is the practical point traditionalists rarely have to answer: the alternative is not celibacy. What I actually get is serial, unstable, unaccountable relationship conducted in the dark, because we have told a person their only options are perfection or exile, and exile is easier. A discipline that says *you will be faithful, you will be permanent, you will be publicly accountable, you will let us hold you to vows* is a **higher** sexual ethic than the one I was enforcing. It asks more, not less. It is covenant applied rather than withheld.

## III. Celibacy Is a Charism, Not a Conscription

Matthew 19:11-12 is the hinge. "Not everyone can receive this saying, but only those to whom it is given." Jesus is explicit that the celibate life is a *given* thing, distributed unevenly. Paul agrees: "each has his own gift from God, one of one kind and one of another" (1 Cor 7:7). And then verse 9, apostolic realism: better to marry than to burn.

The traditional position requires me to tell a category of people, identified by an orientation they did not choose and cannot reliably change, that God has certainly given every one of them a gift Jesus says is not given to everyone. There is no warrant for that claim about the distribution of divine gifts, and much contrary evidence.

The strongest counterargument deserves respect: many straight Christians are involuntarily celibate too — the never-married woman at forty-five, the man whose wife has advanced dementia. Am I not granting one group an exception I deny another?

No, and the difference is structural. They are celibate by *circumstance*, inside a church that prays for their marriage and would rejoice at their wedding. What the traditional position asks of the gay Christian is celibacy that is *categorical and permanent by rule* — the door is not merely closed; it is declared never to have existed. Circumstance is providence. A rule is the church's own act, and the church must answer for it.

And I say this clearly, because he is in my pews and reading this: the celibate gay member who chose that path in obedience has not wasted his life, and I will not let anyone treat his obedience as naïveté or as a mistake now corrected. His celibacy, freely offered, is a genuine charism and an honor to the body. What I refuse is the conscription of everyone else into it.

## IV. Whether the Church Has Ever Revised a Reading

Yes, repeatedly — and each time the traditionalists of the day held the better proof texts.

On slavery, the pro-slavery exegetes had Philemon, Ephesians 6, 1 Timothy 6, and the plain sense; the abolitionists had trajectory, the image of God, and Galatians 3:28. They were right, and the shift was deeper obedience, not capitulation. On usury, a millennium-long prohibition rested on explicit texts and was revised. On divorce and remarriage, most conservative Protestant churches now permit what Jesus appears to prohibit in Mark 10, on pastoral grounds — and do not think themselves unfaithful. On women in ministry, 1 Timothy 2:12 could not be plainer, and half the evangelical world has concluded it is not a universal ordinance.

Where the analogies break down: slavery has no positive scriptural endorsement — it is regulated, never instituted or blessed — whereas male-female marriage is instituted in Genesis 2 and cited by Jesus. That is a real disanalogy and I do not wave it away. The divorce parallel is strongest, precisely because there the church revised a *dominical* teaching on pastoral grounds. And my conclusion is modest: the church has revised rightly and wrongly both. That revision happens proves no particular revision correct. It only refutes the claim that a long consensus is self-authenticating.

## V. How I Would Actually Lead

Not by announcement. I have watched pastors do it that way; it is spiritual malpractice.

**Teaching first, over eighteen months** — and not on this. Eighteen months on how we read Scripture at all: authority, genre, canonical development, how Acts 15 works, how the church has handled disputed matters. People cannot evaluate a conclusion whose method they were never taught.

**Listening second, and structurally.** Facilitated groups mixed by conviction, where the traditionalist elder and the gay couple sit in the same room and each must state the other's position to the other's satisfaction before either may argue. Slow, and the only thing I have seen work.

**Dissent as an office, not a nuisance.** I would name two respected traditionalists and ask them to make the strongest case against me, publicly, from my pulpit, with my support. If my position cannot survive that, it should not survive.

**Not unanimity — but not a bare majority either.** No movement without a supermajority of elders and a long season of consultation. A 5-4 vote on this is not a decision; it is a schism with a date on it.

**Conscience protection in both directions, written into governing documents rather than promised verbally.** No officiant is ever required to perform a wedding against conscience. No member is required to affirm; membership is confession of Christ, not agreement with me. A traditionalist family must be able to raise children here, teach, and hold office. If my change makes this church unlivable for them, I have not led — I have won.

**For those who cannot follow me:** help them leave well — by name, with a blessing, a letter of transfer, a call to the pastor receiving them. The elderly couple leaving after fifty years have a claim on my honor, not my resentment. I will be at their funerals if they will have me.

**To my denomination:** transparency before action. If I am moving out of step with my ordination vows I say so to my overseers first, in writing, and accept the process and its outcome. I will not smuggle. If the price is my credential, that is a price, not an injustice — I made vows. What I will not do is hold the office while quietly teaching against it.

## VI. The Cost, and the Charge

I expect to lose members and to be told I have capitulated to the culture. That is the serious charge, so let me answer it.

If I were reading the culture I would have arrived here twenty years earlier and at far less cost. I resisted this conclusion for most of my ministry, and for scriptural reasons. What moved me was not a poll but Matthew 19:11, Acts 15, and a decade of watching a teaching produce a harvest Galatians 5 does not recognize. Culture and text can point the same direction without the text being overruled; Wilberforce was not less biblical because abolition became popular. And the genetic argument cuts both ways — my traditionalist elders inherited their view as I inherited mine. The question is not who has a social context, but who is reading the whole canon better.

## What Keeps Me Up At Night

**That I may be leading people into sin with the authority of my office.** James 3:1 — not many should become teachers, for we will be judged with greater strictness. If I am wrong I am not merely wrong; I have used ordained authority to lay a stumbling block before the very people I claimed to protect, and done it in God's name. There is no version where I get to say I meant well. Anyone in my position who does not feel that verse physically is not paying attention.

**That "fruit" is a slow and treacherous test.** Fruit ripens over generations, and I am reading a twenty-year sample with my thumb on the scale. Sin's early fruit is frequently sweet — that is the whole mechanism of temptation. An error's bad fruit might not surface until my grandchildren, and I will not be here to be corrected by it. I have also weighted the suffering I witnessed above the suffering I did not, precisely the bias a pastor is most prone to.

**That every accommodation in history felt compassionate at the time.** Prosperity teaching was born of compassion for the poor. Those who liberalized in the 1960s on every question in sight believed they were being merciful, and much of what they built now stands empty. "This is pastorally necessary" is the most-used justification for doctrinal drift in the history of the church, and I am using it.

**That I would be tearing the body.** The global church — Africa, Asia, Latin America, the Orthodox and Catholic communions, the overwhelming majority of Christians alive and of those who ever lived — holds the traditional view. Mine is the position of a wealthy North Atlantic minority, and I know how that pattern has looked every previous time. When my Nigerian brother tells me I am doing colonialism again with better manners, I have no comfortable answer. A rupture with the global body is not a policy disagreement; it is a wound in the thing Christ prayed would be one.

I hold my position, with fear and trembling. And I want to be watched closely by the people who disagree with me — which means I need them to stay.
